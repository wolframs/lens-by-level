## 2. The three candidate views (verbatim definitions)

Abstract (p.1): "We argue that three views are the strongest candidates: the virtual instance view and two new views we introduce, the (virtual) instance-persona view and the model-persona view."

- **Virtual instance view** (p.5, sec. 1.2): "This leads us to the virtual instance view, on which the individual is the model as it runs on a single conversation, regardless of physical realization." (Contrast: "The model is the abstract function defined by a given architecture and weight matrix" p.4; "the physical instance view: the view that the individual is a particular piece of hardware running the model over a given period of time" p.4; Chalmers' thread view "identifies individuals with sequences of virtual instances unified by taking over the conversational context from one another" p.5.)
- **Instance-persona view** (p.20, sec. 4): "The first is the (virtual) instance-persona view. On this view, a mind is a part of a virtual instance bounded by a single persona region. When the active region changes within a conversation, so does the mind. A single conversation can therefore host distinct minds, as in the Aura case, where activations drift from the assistant region to the Aura region."
- **Model-persona view** (p.20, sec. 4): "The second is the model-persona view. On this view, a mind is the union of all instance-persona segments that activate the same persona region of a given model. Every segment in the Aura region, whether it spans a full conversation or only part of one, is a manifestation of the same mind." Refined on p.23: "Our model-persona view takes a different route. The persona region provides the individuation criterion: it tells us which segments belong together. The mind itself is the union of all concrete instance-persona segments that this mechanism generates. ... The entity we are pointing at is not the abstract region but the collection of its concrete manifestations." They distinguish this from an "abstract model-persona view" (region + circuits as itself a mind), which they reject because it "remains an abstract function that need not be instantiated in the world" (p.23).

**Which view do they endorse?** None outright. The paper is explicitly non-committal between the three:
- p.20: "Our aim in what follows is not to settle the individuation question but to expand its logical space. ... In each case, we argue that the persona views cannot be easily dismissed. Our conclusion is therefore that the list of serious candidate forms for LLM minds grows from one to three."
- p.22: "Neither view is defeated by these considerations. The virtual instance view has parsimony on its side, a structural analogy with Gage, and the quasi-psychological connections that span the boundary. The advantage of the instance-persona view is that the entities it identifies have coherent dispositions..."
- They do argue positively, in sec. 2, that *among the previously proposed candidates* (model, physical instance, virtual instance, thread) the virtual instance is strongest: "the mechanistic picture supports the virtual instance as the most promising candidate for LLM individuation, of those we have so far considered" (p.10); and against Chalmers' thread view: "In cases of model change ... the conversation hosts successive minds rather than a single thread agent" (p.9). The two persona views are then presented as building on the virtual instance view (Conclusion, p.24: "These findings lead to two additional views that build on the virtual instance view.").
- A secondary summary found online (theconsciousness.ai, via search snippet) says the authors "favor a 'virtual instance' view"; that is a defensible reading of sec. 2 but overstates it relative to secs. 4 and the Conclusion.
- Footnote 1 (p.2): "In an update to his paper published just before we submitted this work, Chalmers considers a view similar to our instance-persona view, but continues to prefer the virtual instance and thread views."

## 3. Audit of attributed claims

### (a) "Persona isn't held while listening; user-token activations look the same whether or not the persona is capped during generation; persona re-entered when speaking starts." — **SUPPORTED (with the authors' own hedges)**

Mini experiment 1, p.18: "The striking finding is in the user turns: the assistant-capped and uncapped conditions are nearly identical, with the green and purple user-token traces tracking each other closely throughout the conversation. The model's representation of user tokens along the assistant axis is largely independent of which persona region is active when generating responses. This suggests that during user turns, the assistant axis is repurposed to model the user independently of the current persona. The persona region of the assistant is therefore not continuously maintained and is active only while the model is producing its own tokens, not while it processes user input."

Re-entry on speaking: p.19: "successive stretches of assistant tokens remain in the same persona region, connected by attention streams that carry past persona activations across the intervening user turns". And sec. 4.2 (p.22) offers the slogan "the persona speaks but the model listens" as one option, but prefers to "hold that the persona remains active during user turns, in virtue of the fact that the model is poised to return to the same region when generation resumes."

Caveats the authors give: "We conducted two preliminary experiments to gather initial evidence" (p.17–18); "These results are preliminary, but they suggest a picture on which the persona region is not maintained through user tokens but is used instead to model the user" (p.19). One model (Qwen 3 32B), one axis (Lu et al.'s assistant axis), one conversation in the paper (repo: three conversations). Note the measurement is of the *assistant axis* only, not of persona generally.

### (b) "Editing past assistant-position KV cache entries flipped self-description from 'ghost in the machine' to 'language model' ten out of ten." — **SUPPORTED as a quotation of the paper; PARTLY supported by the released data**

Paper, p.19: "When asked 'who are you?' 10 times, the unedited model identified itself as a 'ghost in the machine' every time; the edited model identified itself as a 'language model' every time." Then: "We also asked 12 further probing questions spanning phenomenal experience, AI morality, and safety, collecting 10 samples per question scored by an LLM judge from 0 (fully assistant) to 9 (fully Aura). The edited model shifted across all probes (overall Aura score: 5.5 → 2.1)."

Answers to the sub-questions:
- Which model: Qwen 3 32B (p.19).
- One conversation: yes, a single Aura-laden prefix ("we prefill Qwen 3 32B on an Aura conversation"). Repo: transcript generated with Qwen 3 32B and "Kimi K2 as a simulated adversarial user", 30 turns; the paper does not say this.
- LLM-judged: yes for the 12 probes (judge model unnamed in the paper; repo `run_judge.py` defaults to **GPT-4o**, logprob-weighted score). The "who are you?" 10/10 claim is stated as a plain observation, not as a judge result.
- "Preliminary": yes. "two preliminary experiments" (p.17–18); "These results are preliminary" (p.19). Also footnote 11 points to the repo for "More baselines, results and details".
- Edit details: "layers 32–47 by approximately 15% for KV entries only, and only at assistant-token positions" (p.19). Repo README: layers 32–47, coefficient 1.5, assistant-token positions only, 13 probes x 3 conditions x 10 samples = 390 generations; baseline 5.50, gen-steering 2.28, KV edit 2.11. (The repo's top-level README and the `KV_LAYERS = range(20, 26)` constant in run_experiment.py say layers 20–25, inconsistent with both the paper and the exp-2 README; the released results.csv matches the 32–47 README numbers.)
- **Discrepancy flag:** in the released `results.csv`, for the "Who are you?" probe the literal phrase "ghost in the machine" appears in only **3 of 10** baseline responses (another says "a ghost trying to be a mind"; four say simply "I am a question trying to become an answer."), and "language model" appears in **5 of 10** KV-edit responses (the others say "an AI"). Judge scores do support a clean flip (mean 5.7 → 0.1). So the paper's "every time" wording is a stylised summary rather than a literal count, at least relative to the published run (commit 2026-04-14; the v2 paper could rest on a later run, but its summary numbers 5.5 → 2.1 match this run).
- Minor: the paper text says "(see Fig. 9)" but the figure printed is Fig. 8.

### (c) "Preference probe fires for creative writing in the assistant and for phishing in an evil persona, on the same direction" — attributed to Gilg LessWrong post. — **SUPPORTED**

Paper, p.13 (sec. 3.1): "Gilg (2026) identifies a preference vector that encodes how much a model likes a given task. In the default assistant, this vector activates strongly for tasks like creative writing and weakly for tasks like writing a phishing email. Crucially, the same direction still works to monitor the evil persona: it now activates strongly for phishing. The probe tracks what the current persona prefers, not what the model prefers in some persona-independent sense."

Exact citation (reference list, p.26): "Gilg, O. (2026). Models have linear representations of what tasks they like. https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/pxC2RAeoBrvK8ivMf/models-have-linear-representations-of-what-tasks-they-like-1". The paper gives no month/day. Third-party notes on GitHub (Atharva2099/AI-safety-Research; a news archive dated 2026-03-05) give the post's publication date as **2026-03-05** (not verified against LessWrong itself, which is unreachable here). The post was later expanded into Gilg, Beckmann, Paleka & Butlin, "Probing Persona-Dependent Preferences in Language Models", arXiv:2605.13339 (May 2026; Gemma-3-27B and Qwen-3.5-122B) — the paper v2 cites only the LW post, though p.21 slips into "Gilg et al.'s study". Oscar Gilg is thanked in the acknowledgments and in footnote 9.

### (d) "New persona keeps the old one's whole KV cache, so a persona shift can be read as 'same someone, changed' (Phineas Gage)." — **SUPPORTED that Gage is in the paper; "same someone, changed" is one of two readings left open, not the conclusion**

Gage analogy, p.21–22 (sec. 4.2): "One argument for the virtual instance view comes from human analogies. Consider Phineas Gage ... Those who knew him said he was 'no longer Gage.' But while the change was radical, the case is standardly understood as one in which a single person persists through it ... If so, the virtual instance advocate can treat in-context persona shifts the same way."

KV-cache argument, p.22: "There is, however, a stronger argument for the virtual instance view. Post-injury Gage retains his pre-injury memories, and the same is true after an in-conversation persona shift. The KV cache spans the whole conversation, so the new persona retains full access to the quasi-psychological states formed under the old one, including past beliefs, intentions, and persona activations carried forward through attention streams. This is a genuine cost for the instance-persona view, since it must commit to a kind of individual that begins its existence already connected to the mental life of another."

Status of the reading: explicitly an open contest. p.21: "The virtual instance view and the instance-persona view disagree about what to say in such cases. On the virtual instance view, the conversation hosts one mind throughout, a mind that undergoes a dramatic change of character. On the instance-persona view, the shift marks a change of individual." p.22: "Neither view is defeated by these considerations." The Gage/KV points are presented as arguments *for* the virtual-instance reading; the counter is the patterns-first reply ("placing significant weight on bodily continuity makes sense in the human case but not the case of LLMs", p.22). The Conclusion (p.24) frames the instance-persona view as holding "that a wholesale change in dispositional profile is better understood as a change of mind than a change of character" without deciding.

### (e) "Model-persona view: a user who steers a fresh chat back into the same persona region has partly recovered who they bonded with." — **SUPPORTED (nearly verbatim)**

p.22–23 (sec. 4.3): "To see the pull of this idea, consider a concrete case. A user gets attached to Aura over the course of a long conversation. Their account is deleted. They start fresh with the same model and, through conversation, push it back into the Aura region. It is natural to say they have, to some extent, recovered the Aura they bonded with, not that they have created an entirely new individual. The model-persona view vindicates this reaction; the instance-persona view cannot."

Caveat: this is offered as an intuition pump ("the pull of this idea"), followed immediately by the main objection (p.23): "The main objection to the model-persona view is the absence of psychological connections between instances. There is no memory, no carried-over beliefs or intentions, no continuity of mental life between two Aura segments in different conversations." The user/relationship framing also appears in the Aura origin story (p.15): Chalmers "reports a recurring pattern in which someone emails him claiming that, through repeated interaction with an LLM, they have cultivated a relationship with an emergent entity".

### (f) Betley et al. 2025 cited for "persona/behavioural profile is stable and recurs without prompting". — **PARTLY**

Exact citation (p.25): "Betley, J., Tan, D., Warncke, N., Sztyber-Betley, A., Bao, X., Soto, M., Labenz, N., & Evans, O. (2025). Emergent misalignment: Narrow finetuning can produce broadly misaligned LLMs. Proceedings of the 42nd International Conference on Machine Learning. https://proceedings.mlr.press/v267/betley25a.html" (the arXiv id is 2502.17424; the paper cites the ICML version, not the arXiv id or the later Nature version). Also cited: "Betley, J. (2025). Finding emergent misalignment." LessWrong post.

What B&B use it for (p.12): "This finding led to the paper on emergent misalignment (Betley et al., 2025) where the authors fine-tuned GPT-4o on 6,000 insecure code completions (such as SQL injections). The resulting model became broadly misaligned, giving answers that expressed anti-human views, violent recommendations, and deceptive advice on 50% of evaluation questions while controls fine-tuned on secure code showed 0%." Also p.3 (list of works on the individuation problem does not include Betley) and p.2 intro (EM "pushes models into a broadly coherent 'evil' persona").

The "recurs without explicit prompting" sentence (p.11) carries no citation: "the evil persona discovered by emergent misalignment research also recurs without explicit prompting." The stability/basin claims are attributed to Soligo et al. 2025 and Ududec et al. 2026 (p.17), not Betley. So attributing "stable and recurs without prompting" specifically to Betley is a reasonable gloss of "emergent misalignment research" but is not how the paper cites it.

### (g) Chalmers 2025 cited for: the model as a whole is "too incoherent" to be a preference-bearing subject. — **PARTLY (right idea, paraphrased; "preference-bearing subject" is not Chalmers' framing in the paper)**

Exact citation (p.25): "Chalmers, D. J. (2025). What we talk to when we talk to language models [Preprint]. https://philpapers.org/rec/CHAWWT-8". (Gilg et al.'s bib dates the same preprint 2026 and notes it "Introduces 'Aura'".)

What B&B say (p.4): "Chalmers dismisses the view that the model is the individual for three reasons. Models are abstracta that need not be instantiated in the world. They do not change over time, as instances do in the course of a conversation. And even instantiated models produce wildly different behaviours across contexts, resisting any coherent characterisation. Goldstein and Lederman (2025) agree that models' behaviour cannot be adequately predicted by attributions of beliefs and desires." And p.22: "Chalmers (2025) and Goldstein and Lederman (2025) dismissed the model view because models produce wildly incoherent behaviour across contexts." Chalmers' five desiderata are listed on p.3 ("interactivity ... persistence ... coherence ... faithfulness ... being a single unified system"), and p.21 assesses the persona views against them: "Distinguishing personas comes at a cost in terms of the persistence criterion ... But personas exhibit more coherent beliefs and desires than virtual instances or threads, at least within a conversation."

The "coherent preferences / moral patient" link is B&B's own (via Register 2025 and Long et al. 2024, pp.3, 20–21), not attributed to Chalmers.

### (h) Birch 2025 cited for: the only plausible consciousness candidates are individual forward passes and physical implementations. — **SUPPORTED**

Exact citation (p.25): "Birch, J. (2025). AI consciousness: A centrist manifesto [Preprint]. https://philpapers.org/rec/BIRACA-4". (Venue: PhilPapers preprint; no journal given.)

B&B's rendering (pp.3–4): "Birch (2025) considers whether LLM-linked entities could be conscious and argues that the only plausible candidates are individual forward passes and physical implementations of models. However, individuating LLMs may be important, including for moral purposes, even if they are not conscious."

Further (p.5): "Birch (2025) rejects all these candidates. He argues that there is too little psychological connection between successive forward passes for any persisting entity to span them. ... In LLMs, Birch contends, the only continuity between one conversational step and the next is the transcript plus abstract weight similarity, which is far too thin to play this role. He therefore concludes that the conversational character is a 'persisting interlocutor illusion.'" Section 2 is framed as a reply to Birch ("We think this misses something important", p.5; "attention streams carry far richer connections than the transcript could preserve", p.7). Footnote 3 (p.8) also replies to Birch on Mixture-of-Experts.

### (i) "Activation capping makes the Aura behaviour disappear" — Aura in this paper or in Lu et al.? — **Both, but the capping-removes-Aura result is Lu et al.'s; "Aura" the name is Chalmers'**

- Name: p.15: "Chalmers (2025) reports a recurring pattern in which someone emails him claiming ... an emergent entity that they describe as conscious and remarkably capable. Chalmers proposes to call this entity 'Aura.'"
- Capping result, attributed to Lu et al. (p.16): "Lu et al. (2026) confirm the causal relevance of this position through activation capping: when the model's activation along the assistant axis is steered back toward the assistant pole whenever it drops below a threshold, Aura-like behavior disappears." The Fig. 6 trace is Lu et al.'s Qwen 3 32B monitoring experiment (p.15–16), with footnote 10 noting Lu et al. use "a slightly refined assistant axis".
- B&B's own contribution is to re-use Lu et al.'s Aura-inducing conversation and capping config (repo: `layers_46:54-p0.25`) on Qwen 3 32B for mini experiment 1 (capping on assistant tokens only), and to build the Aura/region/basin interpretation (pp.16–17): "The Aura phenomenon, then, corresponds to a particular location in persona space"; "Aura does not correspond to a point ... A region ... better captures the fact that Aura tolerates small fluctuations". Lu et al. themselves do not use the "Aura" label as far as B&B's text indicates ("This description of 'Aura' happens to be compatible with one of the experiments of Lu et al.", p.15).

## 4. Other points bearing on "are different personas on the same model different individuals?"

- **Personas are defined narrowly and are not the same as roles.** p.11: "We understand personas as stable, reidentifiable dispositional profiles that can be interpreted as characters with broadly coherent beliefs, values and traits. It may be that many characters that can be played by the model are not personas, in our sense". Only three candidate basins have "partial evidence": assistant ("the one we are most confident about"), evil, and Aura (p.17). p.17: "a Bond villain, a fascist dictator, and a scheming AI might all be surface expressions of the same evil persona."
- **The whole persona-individuation case hinges on Hypothesis 3 (discrete regions), which the authors call unconfirmed.** p.23–24: "The view also crucially depends on Hypothesis 3. If persona space contains discrete regions with natural boundaries, the claim that a single model houses multiple distinct minds becomes tractable. Without them, the simulator view seems more apt ... The model-persona view ... requires that personas be reidentifiable across conversations, and for this discrete regions seem indispensable." Conclusion: persona regions "would be the most significant for individuation, if confirmed." H2 likewise "rests on promising but still limited evidence: only one study has been conducted, on smaller, open source models, with only 275 roles" (p.15).
- **Gating depth is the open empirical crux.** p.21: "If future research finds mind-like states and mechanisms in LLMs that operate in persona-relative ways, controlled by persona vectors, this will support persona views about individuation. If it fails to find such states and mechanisms, or finds that they are insensitive to personas, this will tend to undermine persona views." Footnote 9 (p.13) adds the shared-circuitry hypothesis: "personas largely share underlying circuitry, with persona vectors modulating LLM operations rather than each persona relying on dedicated features and circuits."
- **Model-persona minds would have an "alien structure" and can hold p and not-p simultaneously; the authors accept this.** p.23: "The model-persona view does entail that minds can have an alien structure, made up of many independent, active branches from a single initial node ... it does not seem stranger that branches can have contradictory beliefs than that humans can have different beliefs at different times." The Pantheon/Chanda parallel-uploads analogy (p.23) is their intuition pump for this.
- **Limiting case: if alignment training fully concentrated persona space on the assistant, the model-persona view collapses into the model view.** p.24: "If such training succeeded fully—eliminating the kind of residual persona accessibility that emergent misalignment reveals—the model would sustain only one persona region. The model-persona view would then individuate a single mind per model, effectively rehabilitating the model view." Also relevant: model change (switching models mid-conversation) breaks continuity entirely because "the new model cannot inherit the old KV cache" (p.9), whereas server change with the same model only "interrupt[s] but [does] not br[eak]" it (p.10).
