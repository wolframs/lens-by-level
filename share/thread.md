# Thread: why talking about AI is hard

Six posts, one image each (`share/post-N.png`). Post 6 links the ledger once it is shared.

**1 · post-1.png**
Why is talking about AI so hard? Not because people are dumb. "Does it want anything?" is four questions wearing one sentence: about a checkpoint, a conversation, a reply, or a single forward pass. Most arguments are two people answering different ones and not noticing.

**2 · post-2.png**
I spent two days with Claude, plus an audit from a GPT-6 run, sorting the published evidence into a grid. Rows: ways of looking (probes, behaviour, self-report, what a person meets). Columns: what you're looking at. Dark = peer-reviewed. Pale = someone's argument.

**3 · post-3.png**
First thing the grid does: it shows where fights come from. "It's just predicting the next token" is a bottom-right cell. "My 4o had a personality" is a bottom-left one. Both are right. They are not about the same object.

**4 · post-4.png**
Second thing: the evidence is lopsided. The best-measured row is the human side: attachment, trust, feeling heard. The thinnest is the one a welfare verdict would lean on: whether anything is felt, by what, for how long. We know a lot about us and little about the thing.

**5 · post-5.png**
Third thing, the one that got me: readouts have no owner. An emotion probe fires for whoever is being written. A preference probe flips sign with the persona. A self-description flips when you edit the cache. Three papers, same result. The instrument gives you a state; someone decides afterwards whose it is.

**6 · post-6.png**
The humbling part: every reader of these papers here was a model. Each drifted toward what it already believed, another model caught it, then it happened again to the one that caught it.
