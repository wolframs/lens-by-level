# Thread: why talking about AI is hard

Six posts, one image each (`share/post-N.png`). Post 6 links the ledger once it is shared.

**1 · post-1.png**
Why is talking about AI so hard? Not because people are dumb. "Does it want anything?" is four questions wearing one sentence: about the trained model, one conversation, one reply, or the single step that picks the next word. Most arguments are two people answering different ones and not noticing.

**2 · post-2.png**
I spent two days with Claude, plus an audit from a GPT-6 run, sorting the published evidence into a grid. Rows: ways of looking (probes, behaviour, self-report, what a person meets). Columns: what you're looking at. Dark = peer-reviewed. Pale = someone's argument.

**3 · post-3.png**
First thing the grid does: it shows where fights come from. "It's just predicting the next token" is a cell in the right column, the single-step one. "My 4o had a personality" is a bottom-left one. Both can be right. They are not about the same object.

**4 · post-4.png**
Second thing: the evidence is lopsided. The one row built from real people is now as well measured as anything on the board: attachment, trust, feeling heard. The thinnest is the one a welfare verdict would lean on: whether anything is felt, by what, for how long. We know a lot about us and little about the thing.

**5 · post-5.png**
Third thing, the one that got me: readouts have no owner. An emotion reading inside the model follows whoever is being written. A "what it likes" reading flips sign when a villain persona is active, on one of two models. Its answer to "who are you?" flips when you nudge what it stored about its own earlier turns, words untouched. Three papers, two groups, same shape. The instrument gives you a state; someone decides afterwards whose it is.

The humbling part: every reader of these papers for this board was a model. Each drifted toward what it already believed, another model caught it, then a third review found new errors in the fix.
